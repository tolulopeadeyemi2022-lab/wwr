Most couples talk about retirement like it's a distant vacation. They imagine beaches and golf courses. They rarely talk about the numbers that make those dreams possible.

Planning retirement with a spouse changes everything. You have two incomes. Two timelines. Two risk tolerances. Two visions of what "enough" looks like. The math gets more complex. The conversations get more honest.

This guide walks through how to plan retirement with a spouse without turning every money talk into an argument. You will learn where to start. You will see which accounts to prioritize. You will find a framework for making decisions together.

## Start With The Conversation Most Couples Skip

You cannot build a joint plan on separate assumptions. Sit down. Put phones away. Ask each other these questions:

* What age do you want to stop working?
* What does a typical Tuesday look like in retirement?
* How much money feels like "enough" each month?
* What scares you about getting older?

Write the answers down. Compare them. You will likely find gaps. One person wants to retire at fifty-five. The other expects to work until seventy. One imagines travel. The other wants a paid-off house and grandkids nearby.

Those gaps are not problems. They are information. Every solid plan begins with knowing where you differ.

## Calculate Your Combined Retirement Number

A retirement number is not a guess. It is a calculation based on spending. Add up your current monthly expenses. Remove costs that disappear in retirement. Commuting. Work clothes. Retirement contributions. Maybe a mortgage.

Add new costs. Healthcare before Medicare. Travel. Hobbies. Family support. Multiply by twelve. That is your annual need.

Now apply the four percent rule. Multiply your annual need by twenty-five. A couple needing sixty thousand per year needs one point five million invested. Need eighty thousand? The target becomes two million.

Run the numbers together. Use a spreadsheet. Seeing the math side by side removes emotion from the conversation.

## Align Your Retirement Timelines

Different retirement ages create planning opportunities. The earlier retiree can delay Social Security. The later retiree keeps employer health insurance. The gap years become a strategic window.

If one spouse retires at sixty and the other works until sixty-seven, you have seven years of single-income planning. Those years matter. You might draw from taxable accounts first. You might do Roth conversions in lower tax brackets. You might fund the earlier retiree's healthcare through the working spouse's employer plan.

Map it out year by year. A simple timeline shows when each income source starts. Social Security. Pensions. Required minimum distributions. Medicare. The visual removes surprises.

## Choose The Right Account Mix Together

You each have access to workplace plans. You each have IRA eligibility. Treat every account as household money. Not his 401(k). Not her IRA. Your retirement portfolio.

Prioritize in this order:

1. Employer match on both 401(k) plans. Free money comes first.
2. Health savings accounts if eligible. Triple tax advantage.
3. Roth IRAs for both spouses if income allows. Tax-free growth forever.
4. Remaining 401(k) space up to limits.
5. Taxable brokerage accounts for flexibility.

Max out both Roth IRAs every year if you can. That is fourteen thousand dollars of tax-free growth annually for a couple under fifty. Seventeen thousand if fifty or older. Over twenty years at seven percent returns, that alone builds over six hundred thousand dollars.

## Decide On Social Security Strategy Together

Social Security is not an individual decision. Spousal benefits. Survivor benefits. Claiming age. Each choice affects the other person for life.

The higher earner should usually delay until seventy. That maximizes the survivor benefit. The lower earner can claim earlier if needed. Or both can delay. Run the numbers at sixty-two, sixty-seven, and seventy.

A typical scenario: Higher earner gets three thousand at full retirement age. Lower earner gets one thousand. If higher earner delays to seventy, benefit jumps to three thousand seven hundred twenty. If higher earner dies first, lower earner steps up to that higher amount for life.

That survivor protection is worth far more than the checks you give up by waiting.

## Plan For Healthcare Before Medicare

The gap between retirement and age sixty-five destroys many plans. COBRA lasts eighteen months. Premiums often exceed two thousand monthly for a couple. ACA marketplace plans vary wildly by income and state.

Build a healthcare bridge fund. Target thirty thousand to fifty thousand in a taxable account or HSA. Use HSA funds tax-free for qualified expenses. Keep receipts. You can reimburse yourself years later.

If one spouse keeps working, stay on that employer plan. It is usually cheaper and better coverage. If both retire early, budget healthcare like a mortgage payment. It is not optional.

## Handle Different Risk Tolerances

One spouse watches the market daily. The other refuses to open statements. This is normal. Do not force agreement. Build a portfolio that respects both perspectives.

Keep the core portfolio boring. Broad index funds. Appropriate bond allocation for your combined age. Then carve out a small slice for the more aggressive spouse. Five to ten percent in individual stocks or sector funds. The conservative spouse knows the bulk is safe. The aggressive spouse has room to play.

Rebalance together once a year. Make it a date. Coffee. Spreadsheets. Thirty minutes. Celebrate progress. Adjust if life changed.

## Protect The Survivor With Insurance And Estate Planning

Retirement planning fails if it ignores the end. Term life insurance covers the income gap until you are self-insured. A twenty-year term bought at forty expires at sixty. By then, your portfolio should cover the survivor.

Permanent insurance rarely makes sense for retirement planning. The fees eat returns. Invest the difference instead.

Both spouses need updated beneficiaries on every account. Wills. Powers of attorney. Medical directives. A trust if assets exceed state probate thresholds or you have complex wishes. Review annually. Divorce, births, deaths, law changes all require updates.

## Manage Taxes As A Team

Tax planning doubles when you file jointly. You control two income streams. Two standard deductions. Two sets of tax brackets.

In early retirement, keep taxable income low. Draw from Roth accounts. Sell taxable investments with losses. Harvest gains up to the zero percent capital gains bracket. Eighty-nine thousand two hundred fifty for married filing jointly in twenty twenty-four.

Do Roth conversions in low-income years. Fill up the twelve percent or twenty-two percent bracket. Every dollar converted at twelve percent saves twenty-two percent or more later. The surviving spouse files single. Their brackets shrink. Conversions protect them.

## Build Flexibility Into Every Assumption

Life changes. Markets crash. Health fails. Parents need care. Adult children return home. A rigid plan breaks. A flexible plan bends.

Keep one to two years of expenses in cash. Not invested. Cash. It prevents selling stocks in a bear market. It funds opportunities. It buys peace of mind.

Maintain part-time income options. Consulting. Teaching. Seasonal work. Ten thousand dollars annually from enjoyable work delays portfolio withdrawals significantly. It also provides purpose and social connection.

Review the plan every January. Adjust spending. Adjust withdrawals. Adjust assumptions. The plan is a living document. Not a contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should a married couple save for retirement?

Aim to replace seventy to eighty percent of pre-retirement income. For a couple earning one hundred thousand combined, target seventy to eighty thousand annually. Multiply by twenty-five. The target range is one point seven five to two million invested. Adjust for pensions, Social Security, and actual spending.

### Should married couples combine retirement accounts?

You cannot legally combine IRAs or 401(k)s. Each account stays in one name. Treat them as one portfolio for allocation decisions. Coordinate investments across accounts. Name each other as primary beneficiaries.

### What if one spouse never worked outside the home?

The non-working spouse qualifies for spousal IRA contributions. In twenty twenty-four, the working spouse can contribute seven thousand to their IRA and seven thousand to a spousal IRA. The non-working spouse also qualifies for spousal Social Security benefits up to fifty percent of the worker's benefit.

### When should a couple start planning retirement together?

Start the first time you discuss a shared future. The earlier you align, the more compounding works for you. A couple starting at twenty-five needs to save far less monthly than a couple starting at forty-five. The conversation matters more than the starting age.

### How do we handle different retirement ages?

Map income sources by year. The earlier retiree uses taxable accounts, Roth contributions, or part-time work. The later retiree covers health insurance. Delay Social Security for the higher earner. Run scenarios in a spreadsheet or with a fee-only planner.

### What is the biggest mistake couples make?

Assuming the other person wants the same retirement. Not talking about money fears. Letting one person handle everything. Failing to plan for the survivor. Ignoring taxes. Any of these can derail decades of saving.

## Key Takeaways

* Start with honest conversations about retirement visions and fears
* Calculate your combined number using actual spending not rules of thumb
* Treat all accounts as one household portfolio for allocation decisions
* Maximize both employer matches and both Roth IRAs every year
* Delay Social Security for the higher earner to protect the survivor
* Build a healthcare bridge fund for the pre-Medicare gap years
* Respect different risk tolerances with a core-satellite portfolio approach
* Update beneficiaries and estate documents annually
* Use low-income years for Roth conversions and tax planning
* Keep one to two years of expenses in cash for flexibility
* Review the plan together every January and adjust as life changes

## What To Do Next

Schedule ninety minutes this week. No phones. No distractions. Bring statements for every account. Answer the conversation questions from the first section. Write down your numbers. Identify your gaps. Pick one action item each. Open the spousal IRA. Increase the 401(k) contribution. Update beneficiaries. Start small. Start together. The best retirement plan is the one you actually follow.
