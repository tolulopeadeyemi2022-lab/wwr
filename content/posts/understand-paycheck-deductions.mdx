Your paycheck arrives. You glance at the direct deposit amount. It looks smaller than expected. Again.

Most people have been there. You know your salary. You know your hourly rate. But the number that actually hits your bank account feels like a mystery. Understanding what comes out of your paycheck isn't just about curiosity. It's about control. When you know exactly where every dollar goes, you can make better decisions about taxes, benefits, and your overall financial plan.

This guide breaks down every common deduction so you can read your pay stub with confidence.

## Why Your Paycheck Looks Different Than Your Salary

Your gross pay is the number on your offer letter. Your net pay is what you actually receive. The gap between them isn't random. It's a series of required and optional deductions that serve specific purposes.

Some deductions are mandatory. Federal income tax. Social Security. Medicare. State and local taxes where they apply. Others are choices you made during open enrollment or when you started the job. Health insurance. Retirement contributions. Life insurance. Flexible spending accounts.

The problem isn't that deductions exist. The problem is that most people never sit down to understand each one. They assume the numbers are correct. They hope the math works out. Then tax season arrives and they're surprised by a bill or a smaller refund than expected.

You don't need a finance degree to figure this out. You need to know what to look for.

## The Required Deductions You Can't Avoid

Every paycheck in the United States has certain deductions by law. These aren't negotiable. Your employer must withhold them and send them to the government on your behalf.

### Federal Income Tax Withholding

This is usually the largest deduction on your pay stub. The amount depends on your filing status, your income level, and the information you provided on Form W-4 when you were hired. The IRS uses tax brackets. As your income rises, the rate on each additional dollar increases.

Your employer calculates withholding using IRS tables. They don't guess. But the system isn't perfect. It assumes standard deductions and typical situations. If you have multiple jobs, a working spouse, or significant deductions, the standard withholding might be too high or too low.

Check your W-4 if you consistently get large refunds or owe money at tax time. A large refund means you gave the government an interest-free loan all year. Owing money means you didn't withhold enough. The goal is to land close to zero.

### Social Security Tax

You'll see this listed as FICA, OASDI, or Social Security on your pay stub. The rate is 6.2 percent on earnings up to the annual wage base limit. For 2024, that limit is $168,600. Earnings above that amount aren't subject to Social Security tax.

Your employer pays an matching 6.2 percent. If you're self-employed, you pay the full 12.4 percent yourself. This funds retirement, disability, and survivor benefits for current recipients. You earn credits toward your own future benefits with every paycheck.

### Medicare Tax

Medicare tax is 1.45 percent on all earnings. No wage base limit applies. Every dollar you earn gets hit with this tax. Your employer matches this 1.45 percent as well.

High earners pay an additional 0.9 percent Medicare surtax on wages above $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married couples filing jointly. This extra tax started in 2013 to help fund the Affordable Care Act. Employers don't match the surtax portion.

### State and Local Income Taxes

Forty-one states have state income tax. Rates vary widely. Some states use flat rates. Others use progressive brackets like the federal system. Nine states have no state income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming.

Some cities and counties add their own income taxes. New York City residents pay city tax on top of state tax. Philadelphia has a wage tax. If you live in one jurisdiction and work in another, you might owe taxes to both. Reciprocal agreements between some states simplify this.

## The Deductions You Choose

These deductions come from decisions you made during benefits enrollment. You can often change them during open enrollment or after qualifying life events like marriage, birth of a child, or loss of other coverage.

### Health Insurance Premiums

If your employer offers health insurance, your share of the premium comes out pre-tax in most cases. That means the deduction reduces your taxable income for federal, state, and FICA purposes. You pay less tax overall.

Premium amounts vary based on the plan you choose. High-deductible health plans typically have lower premiums. PPO plans with lower deductibles cost more per paycheck. Family coverage costs significantly more than individual coverage.

Your employer likely pays a substantial portion of the total premium. The amount you see on your pay stub is only your share. The total cost of your coverage is usually much higher.

### Dental and Vision Insurance

These work similarly to health insurance. Premiums are typically deducted pre-tax. Costs are lower than medical coverage but still meaningful. A family dental plan might cost $30 to $60 per paycheck. Vision plans are often under $10.

### Retirement Contributions

Traditional 401(k) or 403(b) contributions come out pre-tax. If you earn $5,000 per paycheck and contribute 10 percent, your taxable income for that period drops to $4,500. You'll pay income tax on that $500 later when you withdraw it in retirement.

Roth 401(k) contributions work differently. They come out after tax. You pay tax now. Qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. The deduction amount looks the same on your pay stub. The tax treatment is what differs.

Employer matching contributions don't appear as deductions on your pay stub. They're added directly to your retirement account. You'll see them on your 401(k) statement, not your paycheck.

### Life and Disability Insurance

Basic employer-provided life insurance is often free up to a certain amount, typically one times your salary. Supplemental coverage for yourself, your spouse, or your children comes out as a post-tax deduction in most cases.

Short-term and long-term disability insurance premiums can be paid pre-tax or post-tax depending on your employer's plan. If you pay pre-tax, any benefits you receive later are taxable. If you pay post-tax, benefits are tax-free. This choice matters if you ever need to file a claim.

### Flexible Spending Accounts and Health Savings Accounts

FSA contributions for healthcare or dependent care come out pre-tax. You decide the annual amount during open enrollment. It's divided evenly across your paychecks. Use it or lose it rules apply to healthcare FSAs. Dependent care FSAs have a grace period or carryover option.

HSA contributions are also pre-tax. They reduce federal, state, and FICA taxes. Unlike FSAs, HSA funds roll over year to year. You can invest them. They're triple tax-advantaged: contributions are tax-free, growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. You must have a high-deductible health plan to qualify.

### Other Voluntary Deductions

You might see deductions for:
- Union dues
- Charitable giving through payroll deduction
- Commuter benefits for transit or parking
- Employee stock purchase plans
- Loan repayments for 401(k) loans
- Garnishments for child support, tax debts, or court orders

Some of these are pre-tax. Others are post-tax. Your pay stub should indicate which is which.

## How to Read Your Pay Stub Like a Pro

Every pay stub has the same basic sections. Learning to scan them quickly saves time and catches errors.

### Gross Pay Section

This shows your total earnings before any deductions. For salaried employees, it's your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods. For hourly workers, it's hours worked times hourly rate plus any overtime, bonuses, or shift differentials.

Verify the hours. Verify the rate. Mistakes happen. A missed overtime payment or incorrect hour count costs you money.

### Taxes Withheld Section

This lists every tax deduction separately. Federal income tax. Social Security. Medicare. State tax. Local tax if applicable. Each should have a year-to-date column showing cumulative amounts for the calendar year.

Compare the year-to-date amounts to your last pay stub. They should increase by roughly the same amount each period unless your pay changed or you hit a wage base limit.

### Pre-Tax Deductions Section

These reduce your taxable wages. Health insurance. Dental. Vision. Traditional retirement contributions. FSA. HSA. Commuter benefits. The total here lowers the amount subject to federal and state income tax.

### Post-Tax Deductions Section

These come out after taxes are calculated. Roth retirement contributions. Supplemental life insurance. Union dues. Charitable donations. Garnishments. These don't reduce your current tax bill.

### Net Pay Section

This is the final number. Gross pay minus all deductions equals net pay. This should match your direct deposit amount or physical check amount exactly.

### Year-to-Date Totals

Every category should show year-to-date figures. These are essential for tax planning. They help you estimate whether you're on track for a refund or a balance due. They also verify that you're getting credit for all retirement contributions and tax payments.

## Common Paycheck Surprises and What They Mean

### Your First Paycheck of the Year Looks Different

January paychecks often show higher net pay. Why? Social Security tax resets. You start paying 6.2 percent again on the first dollar. If you hit the wage base limit last year, you stopped paying Social Security tax for the rest of the year. January restarts the clock.

Federal withholding might also change if tax brackets or standard deductions were adjusted for inflation. The IRS updates these annually.

### A Bonus or Overtime Check Has Higher Withholding

Supplemental wages like bonuses, commissions, and overtime are often withheld at a flat 22 percent federal rate for amounts up to $1 million. Amounts over $1 million get 37 percent withholding. This doesn't mean you're taxed at a higher rate overall. It's just a withholding method. Your actual tax liability is calculated on your total income when you file your return.

You might see a bigger refund or smaller balance due because of this.

### Your Raise Didn't Increase Your Take-Home Pay Much

Moving into a higher tax bracket only affects the dollars in that bracket. Not your entire income. But a raise can also push you past thresholds for certain deductions or credits. It might reduce eligibility for Roth IRA contributions or certain tax credits. The net effect varies.

Run the numbers. A $5,000 raise might only add $3,000 to your annual take-home pay after taxes. That's still $3,000 more than you had. But budgeting for the full $5,000 leads to disappointment.

### Your Health Insurance Premium Jumped Mid-Year

This usually means a qualifying life event triggered a plan change. Marriage. Divorce. Birth. Adoption. Loss of other coverage. Or your employer changed plans or contribution percentages. They should notify you in advance. If they didn't, ask HR for an explanation.

## How to Check If Your Withholding Is Right

The IRS provides a Tax Withholding Estimator online. It takes about 15 minutes. You'll need your most recent pay stubs, your last tax return, and information about other income sources.

Use it when:
- You start a new job
- You get married or divorced
- You have a child
- You buy a house
- You have significant non-wage income
- You got a large refund or owed a lot last year
- Tax laws change significantly

Adjust your W-4 with your employer if the estimator suggests a change. You can submit a new W-4 anytime. Changes take effect in the next payroll cycle.

## What Your Pay Stub Doesn't Show

Your pay stub shows deductions. It doesn't show the full picture of your compensation.

### Employer-Paid Benefits

Your employer likely pays:
- The employer portion of Social Security and Medicare (7.65 percent)
- Most of your health insurance premium (often 70 to 80 percent)
- Workers' compensation insurance
- Unemployment insurance
- Disability insurance (in some states)
- 401(k) matching contributions
- Life insurance (basic coverage)
- Paid time off accrual value

These don't appear on your pay stub. They're real compensation. A $60,000 salary with good benefits might be worth $75,000 or more in total compensation.

### The Value of Pre-Tax Deductions

Every pre-tax dollar you contribute to retirement or health accounts saves you money at your marginal tax rate. If you're in the 22 percent federal bracket with a 5 percent state tax, every $100 contributed saves you $27 in taxes. That's a 27 percent instant return before any investment growth.

Your pay stub shows the deduction. It doesn't show the tax savings. Calculate it yourself occasionally. It's motivating.

## Practical Steps to Take This Week

Pull up your most recent pay stub. Digital or paper. Spend ten minutes with it.

1. Identify every deduction by name and amount
2. Categorize each as required tax, pre-tax benefit, or post-tax benefit
3. Check the year-to-date totals against your expectations
4. Verify your federal withholding using the IRS estimator
5. Confirm your retirement contribution percentage matches what you intended
6. Make sure your health plan selection matches what you chose during open enrollment
7. Note any deductions you don't recognize and ask HR about them

Do this once per quarter. Do it after any life change. Do it after any raise or bonus. Understanding your paycheck isn't a one-time task. It's a habit that pays dividends.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my federal withholding is correct?

Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov. Input your filing status, income, deductions, and credits. It will tell you if you're on track for a refund, a balance due, or close to zero. Adjust your W-4 if needed.

### Why is my Social Security tax different this month?

You might have hit the annual wage base limit. Once your year-to-date earnings exceed $168,600 for 2024, Social Security tax stops until January. Or you might have had a bonus that pushed you over the limit mid-year.

### Can I stop 401(k) contributions temporarily?

Yes. Contact your HR or payroll department. Most plans allow you to change your contribution percentage at any time. Changes typically take effect in the next pay period. Remember to restart contributions when you can.

### What's the difference between pre-tax and post-tax deductions?

Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income now. You pay tax later (traditional 401(k)) or never (HSA for qualified expenses). Post-tax deductions don't reduce current taxable income. Roth 401(k) contributions are post-tax but grow tax-free.

### Should I choose a high-deductible health plan to lower my paycheck deduction?

Lower premiums mean higher out-of-pocket costs when you need care. If you're generally healthy and have savings to cover the deductible, a high-deductible plan with an HSA can be a smart financial move. If you have ongoing medical needs, a lower-deductible plan might cost less overall despite higher premiums.

### How do I fix an error on my pay stub?

Contact your payroll or HR department immediately. Provide the pay stub and explain the discrepancy. Most errors are simple data entry mistakes. They can usually issue a correction in the next pay cycle or provide a separate adjustment payment.

### What happens to my deductions if I get paid biweekly versus semimonthly?

Biweekly means 26 pay periods per year. Semimonthly means 24. Your annual deduction amounts stay the same. Per-paycheck amounts differ. Biweekly paychecks are slightly smaller. Two months per year have three biweekly paychecks. Budget accordingly.

## Key Takeaways

- Your paycheck gap isn't mysterious. Every deduction has a name, a purpose, and a tax treatment.
- Required taxes include federal income tax, Social Security (6.2 percent up to the wage base), Medicare (1.45 percent on all earnings), and applicable state and local taxes.
- Voluntary deductions for health insurance, retirement, and FSAs are often pre-tax and reduce your current tax bill.
- Roth retirement contributions are post-tax but grow tax-free. Traditional contributions are pre-tax but taxed at withdrawal.
- Check your W-4 whenever your life or tax situation changes. The IRS estimator takes 15 minutes.
- Your pay stub shows year-to-date totals. Use them for tax planning and error detection.
- Employer-paid benefits don't appear on your pay stub but represent significant value.
- Review your pay stub quarterly. Catch mistakes early. Understand every line item.

## What To Do Next

Open your most recent pay stub right now. Don't wait for the weekend. Don't wait for tax season. Spend five minutes identifying each deduction. Circle anything you don't understand. Email HR with questions tomorrow morning.

Then bookmark the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator. Put a calendar reminder for quarterly paycheck reviews. The next time your paycheck arrives, you'll know exactly what you're looking at. That confidence is worth more than the ten minutes it takes to build it.
