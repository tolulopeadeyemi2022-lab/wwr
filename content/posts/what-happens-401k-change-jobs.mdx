You stare at the resignation letter on your desk. The new offer sits beside it with better pay and better benefits. Then a thought hits. What happens to the 401k you built over three years? Most people don't know the answer until they're forced to find out. The good news is you have choices. The bad news is one of them could cost you thousands.

Your 401k doesn't disappear when you leave. It stays right where it sits unless you move it. But leaving it behind isn't always the smartest move. Let's walk through every option so you can decide with confidence.

## Your Four Main Options

When you separate from an employer, your 401k typically gives you four paths forward. Each has distinct advantages and drawbacks depending on your situation.

### Leave It With Your Old Employer

You can keep the account exactly where it is. This works if you like the investment options and fees are low. Many large companies offer institutional-class funds with expense ratios under 0.10 percent. That's cheaper than most IRAs.

But you lose the ability to contribute. You also can't take a loan against the balance. And if your balance drops below five thousand dollars, the plan can force you out. They'll either cut a check with twenty percent withheld or roll it into an IRA of their choosing. Neither outcome is ideal.

### Roll It Into Your New Employer's Plan

If your new job offers a 401k with good funds, this consolidates everything in one place. You keep the creditor protection that employer plans provide. You may also qualify for loans if the new plan allows them.

The catch? Not all plans accept incoming rollovers. Some require a waiting period before you can participate. And you're stuck with whatever investment menu the new plan offers. If those funds carry high fees, you're locked in.

### Roll It Into an IRA

This is often the most flexible choice. An IRA at Vanguard, Fidelity, or Schwab gives you access to thousands of low-cost funds and ETFs. You control every investment decision. You can also convert to a Roth IRA if that fits your tax strategy.

The trade-off is losing creditor protection in some states. Federal law protects 401k assets from bankruptcy. IRA protection varies by state. You also can't take loans from an IRA. And if you're between fifty-five and fifty-nine and a half, you lose the rule of fifty-five that lets you withdraw from a 401k penalty-free after leaving an employer.

### Cash It Out

This is almost always the wrong choice. You'll owe income tax plus a ten percent early withdrawal penalty if you're under fifty-nine and a half. On a fifty-thousand-dollar balance, that's roughly fifteen thousand dollars gone to taxes and penalties. You also kill the compound growth that money would have earned for decades.

I've seen people cash out thirty thousand dollars to pay off credit cards. Five years later they have no retirement savings and the cards are maxed again. Don't do it.

## How To Compare Your Options

Let's look at a real scenario. Sarah has eighty thousand dollars in her old 401k. The plan charges 0.45 percent in administrative fees on top of fund expenses. Her new employer's plan uses Vanguard institutional funds with total costs around 0.07 percent. An IRA at Vanguard would cost about the same.

If she leaves it, she pays roughly three hundred sixty dollars a year in extra fees. Over twenty years that's over seven thousand dollars lost to fees alone. Rolling to the new 401k or an IRA saves that money. The IRA gives her more fund choices. The new 401k keeps her loan option open.

The math changes with every situation. A fifty-thousand-dollar balance in a cheap plan might be fine staying put. A two-hundred-thousand-dollar balance in an expensive plan should almost always move.

## The Rollover Process Step By Step

Moving your money isn't complicated. But doing it wrong creates tax headaches.

First, decide where the money goes. Open the destination account if it doesn't exist. For an IRA, you'll need a traditional IRA for pre-tax money or a Roth IRA if you're converting.

Second, contact your old plan administrator. Ask for a direct rollover form. Specify that you want a trustee-to-trustee transfer. This means the check goes directly to the new custodian. Never have them send a check to you. If they do, twenty percent gets withheld for taxes. You'd have to replace that twenty percent from your own pocket within sixty days to avoid penalties.

Third, submit the paperwork. The old plan will liquidate your investments and send the cash. This takes one to three weeks. The money sits in cash during transfer so you're out of the market briefly.

Fourth, confirm the money arrived. Log into the new account. Verify the amount matches. Then invest according to your plan.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Money

People make the same errors repeatedly. Here are the big ones.

Taking a distribution instead of a direct rollover. The twenty percent withholding trap catches thousands every year. You have sixty days to deposit the full amount including the withheld portion. Miss the window and it's a taxable distribution.

Rolling Roth 401k money into a traditional IRA. Roth contributions should go to a Roth IRA. Mixing them creates a tracking nightmare. You'll pay taxes again on money that should stay tax-free.

Forgetting about the money entirely. I know someone who left twelve thousand dollars in a 401k from a job she held in 2014. She forgot it existed. The plan eventually forced it into a high-fee IRA she didn't choose. That money could have grown to thirty thousand by now.

Not checking vesting schedules. Your employer match may not be fully yours. Leaving before you're vested means leaving free money on the table. Check your plan documents before you quit if possible.

## When You Should Move Fast

Some situations demand quick action.

If your old plan balance is under five thousand dollars, the plan can force a distribution at any time. They don't need your permission. Get ahead of it.

If the plan is terminating or the company is being acquired, decisions happen on their timeline. Stay informed.

If you're approaching age fifty-five and might need early access, the rule of fifty-five only works with your most recent employer's 401k. Rolling to an IRA kills that option.

## What About Outstanding Loans

If you borrowed from your 401k, the loan typically comes due when you leave. You usually have sixty to ninety days to repay the full balance. If you can't, the remaining amount becomes a taxable distribution. That triggers taxes and penalties.

Some new employers allow you to roll the loan balance into their plan. This is rare but worth asking about. Otherwise you need a plan to repay or accept the tax hit.

## The Tax Implications You Need To Know

Pre-tax 401k money stays pre-tax in a traditional IRA or new 401k. No taxes due on a direct rollover.

Roth 401k money stays Roth in a Roth IRA. No taxes due.

Converting pre-tax money to a Roth IRA triggers income tax on the converted amount. This can make sense in low-income years. But you need cash outside the account to pay the tax bill. Don't withhold from the conversion. That defeats the purpose.

After-tax contributions in your 401k can be rolled to a Roth IRA tax-free. The earnings on those contributions go to a traditional IRA. This is called the mega backdoor Roth strategy. It's powerful but complex. Talk to a tax professional before attempting it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do I have to decide what to do with my 401k?

There's no deadline for balances over five thousand dollars. The money can stay indefinitely. But under five thousand dollars, the plan can force you out at any time. For forced distributions, you have sixty days to roll over if they send a check.

### Can I roll my 401k into a Roth IRA?

Yes. This is a Roth conversion. You'll owe income tax on the pre-tax portion. The ten percent penalty doesn't apply to conversions. But you need outside money to pay the tax. Doing it in a year when your income is lower reduces the tax impact.

### What happens to my employer match when I leave?

You keep whatever is vested. Unvested portions are forfeited back to the plan. Vesting schedules vary. Some are immediate. Others take three to six years. Check your summary plan description.

### Can I keep multiple 401ks from different jobs?

Yes. There's no limit on how many 401k accounts you can have. But managing multiple accounts gets messy. Consolidating simplifies your life and often reduces fees.

### What if my new employer doesn't offer a 401k?

An IRA becomes your best option. You can still contribute up to seven thousand dollars annually (eight thousand if you're fifty or older). That's less than the twenty-three thousand 401k limit, but it keeps you saving.

### Does rolling over count toward my annual contribution limit?

No. Rollovers are not contributions. They don't affect your annual limit at all. You can roll over five hundred thousand dollars and still contribute the full amount to your new 401k or IRA.

## Key Takeaways

Your 401k stays intact when you change jobs. You control what happens next.

Direct rollovers avoid taxes and penalties. Never take a check made out to you.

Compare fees before deciding. High fees eat returns over decades.

IRAs offer more investment choices. Employer plans offer better creditor protection and loan options.

Cashing out costs roughly thirty to forty percent in taxes and penalties. Don't do it.

Outstanding 401k loans come due when you leave. Plan for repayment.

Vesting schedules determine how much employer match you keep. Check before you quit.

## What To Do Next

Log into your old 401k today. Download your latest statement. Note the balance, vesting status, and any outstanding loan.

Call the plan administrator. Ask about rollover procedures and fees. Get the direct rollover form.

Open a destination account if you don't have one. A traditional IRA at a low-cost broker works for most people.

Submit the paperwork. Track the transfer. Invest the money once it arrives.

Then set a calendar reminder to review your retirement accounts once a year. One hour of maintenance prevents years of neglect. Your future self will thank you.
