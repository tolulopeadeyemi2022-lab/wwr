Most people buy life insurance because someone told them they should. Few people actually know if the amount they bought makes sense for their situation.

You pay the premiums every month. You hope your family never needs to use it. But have you ever sat down and calculated whether your coverage would actually replace your income for the right number of years?

If you're asking how much life insurance do I need, you're already ahead of most people. The answer isn't a random multiple of your salary. It's not whatever your employer offers. It's a specific number based on what your family would actually need if you weren't there tomorrow.

Let's figure out your real number.

## Why Most People Get This Wrong

The old rule of thumb says buy ten times your income. That sounds smart until you actually do the math.

A software engineer earning $120,000 with a paid-off house and a spouse who also works might need $500,000. A single parent earning $60,000 with a mortgage, two kids, and no other savings might need $1.5 million.

Same income. Completely different needs.

The multiple-of-income rule ignores your debts. It ignores your assets. It ignores whether your spouse works. It ignores how old your kids are. It ignores whether you have aging parents who depend on you.

Insurance agents love the simple rule because it sells bigger policies. You need something more precise.

## The DIME Method Gives You a Real Number

DIME stands for Debt, Income, Mortgage, and Education. Add those four numbers together. That's your coverage target.

**Debt** means every loan except your mortgage. Car loans. Student loans. Credit cards. Personal loans. Medical debt. If you owe $28,000 across these, that's your starting number.

**Income** means how many years your family would need your salary replaced. A common approach: multiply your annual income by the number of years until your youngest child turns 18. If you earn $75,000 and your youngest is 4, that's 14 years. 14 times $75,000 equals $1,050,000.

**Mortgage** is your current payoff balance. Not the original loan amount. The actual balance today. If you owe $245,000, add that.

**Education** estimates college costs for each child. A reasonable baseline: $100,000 per child for four years at a public university. Two kids means $200,000.

Let's total our example:
* Debt: $28,000
* Income replacement: $1,050,000
* Mortgage: $245,000
* Education: $200,000
* **Total: $1,523,000**

Round up to $1.5 million or $1.75 million for a cushion. That's your number.

## Subtract What You Already Have

Now subtract assets your family could use immediately.

* Existing life insurance (employer policy, any personal policies)
* Savings and investments they'd access
* Retirement accounts (though these have tax implications)
* Home equity beyond the mortgage payoff
* Any other liquid assets

Don't count illiquid assets like a business or collectibles. Don't count assets your family would need to keep, like the primary vehicle.

If our example family has $150,000 in savings, a $200,000 employer policy, and $100,000 in accessible investments, that's $450,000 in existing resources.

$1,523,000 minus $450,000 equals $1,073,000 in new coverage needed.

Round to $1.1 million. Buy a $1.1 million term policy.

## Term Life Is Almost Always the Right Choice

Whole life insurance has a place for high-net-worth estate planning. For 95 percent of people, term life is the correct tool.

Term life covers you for a specific period. 10, 20, or 30 years. You pay a fixed premium. If you die during the term, your beneficiaries get the death benefit. If you outlive the term, the policy expires.

A healthy 35-year-old male can buy a 20-year, $1 million term policy for roughly $45 to $60 per month. A whole life policy for the same amount might cost $800 to $1,200 per month.

The difference is $900 to $1,100 monthly. Invested in a low-cost index fund at 8 percent average returns, that difference grows to roughly $500,000 to $600,000 over 20 years.

Buy term. Invest the difference. That's not a slogan. It's math.

## Match the Term to Your Obligations

Your policy should last until your major financial obligations end.

* Kids through college? 20 or 30 years.
* Mortgage payoff in 15 years? 20-year term covers it.
* Spouse reaches retirement age in 12 years? 15 or 20 years.

Don't buy a 30-year term if your obligations end in 15. You're paying for coverage you won't need.

Don't buy a 10-year term if your youngest is 3. You'll be uninsurable or face skyrocketing premiums when the term ends.

## Employer Coverage Is a Bonus, Not a Plan

Group life through work is nice. It's usually one or two times your salary. Sometimes you can buy supplemental coverage at group rates.

Three problems with relying on it:

1. **You lose it when you leave the job.** Most group policies aren't portable. If you quit, get laid off, or retire, the coverage vanishes.
2. **Health changes trap you.** If you develop a condition while covered by work, you can't get affordable individual coverage later.
3. **It's rarely enough.** Two times a $90,000 salary is $180,000. That covers funeral costs and a few months of bills. Not years of income replacement.

Keep the employer coverage. Buy your own policy on top of it.

## Stay-at-Home Parents Need Coverage Too

A stay-at-home parent provides childcare, household management, transportation, meal planning, and emotional labor that would cost real money to replace.

The average cost of full-time childcare for two kids exceeds $25,000 per year in many states. Add housekeeping, meal prep, logistics, and the mental load of running a household. The economic value often exceeds $75,000 to $100,000 annually.

If the stay-at-home parent dies, the working parent either pays for replacement services or reduces work hours. Both cost money.

A $500,000 to $1 million term policy on a stay-at-home parent costs $20 to $40 per month. It's one of the highest-ROI financial decisions a family can make.

## Recalculate Every Major Life Change

Your coverage needs shift when:

* You have another child
* You buy a house
* You pay off your mortgage
* Your spouse changes jobs or income
* You start a business
* You inherit money
* Your kids graduate college
* You divorce

Set a calendar reminder to review your coverage annually. It takes 15 minutes. The cost of being wrong is your family's financial security.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Families

**Buying whole life because the agent said it's an investment.** It's not. The returns are low. The fees are high. The surrender charges are punitive. Keep insurance and investing separate.

**Naming minor children as direct beneficiaries.** They can't receive the money until a court appoints a guardian. Name a trust or a trusted adult as beneficiary instead.

**Forgetting to update beneficiaries after divorce.** Your ex-spouse could receive the payout if you never changed the form. Check every policy after major life events.

**Lying on the application.** Insurers investigate claims during the contestability period (usually two years). Material misrepresentations void the policy. Your family gets nothing.

**Buying too little because the premium felt high.** A $250,000 policy costs $15 monthly. A $1 million policy costs $45 monthly. The difference is $360 per year. The difference in coverage is $750,000. Don't be penny-wise and pound-foolish.

## How to Shop Without Getting Sold

Get quotes from at least three independent sources:

* Policygenius
* Haven Life
* Ladder
* Ethos
* A local independent agent who represents multiple carriers

Compare the same term length and coverage amount. Look at the financial strength ratings (AM Best, Moody's, S&P). Choose a carrier rated A or better.

Don't buy riders you don't understand. The accelerated death benefit rider (lets you access funds if terminally ill) is usually free and worth having. The waiver of premium rider (pays premiums if disabled) adds cost and has strict definitions. Skip it unless you have a specific reason.

Apply online. Take the paramedical exam (blood pressure, blood draw, urine sample). It takes 20 minutes at your home or office. Honest answers get you the best rate.

## What If You're Uninsurable?

Some health conditions make traditional term life expensive or unavailable.

Options exist:

* **Guaranteed issue life insurance.** No medical exam. Small coverage amounts ($25,000 to $50,000). Graded death benefit (limited payout in first two years). Expensive per dollar of coverage. Better than nothing.
* **Simplified issue life insurance.** Health questions but no exam. Up to $500,000 coverage. Higher premiums than fully underwritten policies.
* **Group life through professional associations.** Some organizations offer member rates with relaxed underwriting.
* **Accidental death and dismemberment (AD&D) policies.** Only pays for accidents. Not a substitute for life insurance but adds some protection.

Work with an independent agent who specializes in impaired risk cases. They know which carriers underwrite your condition favorably.

## The Conversation You Need to Have

Buying the policy is step one. Step two is making sure your family can actually use it.

Tell your spouse or trusted person:
* Which company holds the policy
* The policy number
* Where you keep the physical or digital documents
* The contact information for your agent (if you have one)
* That they should call the claims department immediately, not the agent

Create a simple one-page document with this information. Store it with your will and other important papers. Review it annually.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much life insurance do I need if I'm single with no dependents?

You probably don't need any. Life insurance replaces income for people who depend on you. If no one relies on your income, your money is better spent on disability insurance, retirement savings, or an emergency fund. Exception: if you have co-signed debt that would burden someone else, a small policy covering that specific debt makes sense.

### Should I buy life insurance on my children?

Almost never. Children don't provide income. The financial impact of a child's death is funeral costs, which a small emergency fund covers. Agents sell child riders as "locking in insurability," but the amount is tiny ($10,000 to $25,000) and the cost over years exceeds the benefit. Put that money in a 529 plan instead.

### What happens if I outlive my term policy?

The policy expires. You stop paying premiums. You get nothing back. That's the design. Term insurance is pure protection, not an investment. If you still need coverage at that point (unlikely if you planned correctly), you can apply for a new policy at your older age and health status. This is why buying the right term length matters.

### Can I have multiple life insurance policies?

Yes. You can stack policies from different companies. Some people buy a 20-year policy for mortgage coverage and a 30-year policy for income replacement until retirement. This can be cheaper than one large 30-year policy. Just disclose existing coverage on each application.

### Does life insurance pay out for suicide?

Most policies have a two-year suicide exclusion. If the insured dies by suicide within the first two years, the company refunds premiums paid but doesn't pay the death benefit. After two years, the full benefit pays out. This varies by state and carrier. Read your policy.

## Key Takeaways

* Calculate your real need using Debt, Income, Mortgage, and Education, then subtract existing assets.
* Term life insurance is the right tool for almost everyone. Whole life serves a narrow estate-planning niche.
* Match your term length to your longest financial obligation. Don't overbuy or underbuy the duration.
* Employer coverage is supplemental. Own a personal policy that stays with you.
* Stay-at-home parents need coverage equal to the cost of replacing their labor.
* Recalculate annually and after every major life change.
* Shop multiple carriers. Compare identical term lengths and coverage amounts.
* Make sure your beneficiaries know the policy exists and where to find it.

## What To Do Next

1. **Gather your numbers.** Pull your debt balances, mortgage payoff amount, and income. Estimate education costs per child.
2. **Run the DIME calculation.** Add the four categories. Subtract liquid assets and existing coverage.
3. **Get three quotes.** Use Policygenius, Haven Life, and one independent agent for the same term and amount.
4. **Apply for the best offer.** Schedule the paramedical exam. Answer honestly.
5. **Create the one-page policy summary.** Give it to your spouse or trusted person. Store a copy with your will.
6. **Set a calendar reminder.** Review coverage annually on the same date each year.

You're not buying life insurance because you expect to die soon. You're buying it because you love people who would struggle without you. The right amount lets them grieve without worrying about the mortgage. That's worth 15 minutes of math and a few dollars a month.
