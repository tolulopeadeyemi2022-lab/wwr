You opened your 401k portal and saw a contribution limit notification. Now you're wondering if you're leaving money on the table. This happens to almost everyone at some point.

Retirement account contribution limits change almost every year. The IRS adjusts them for inflation. Missing an increase means missing free tax advantages. Hitting the wrong limit triggers penalties. Let's sort through the numbers so you can make the most of every account.

## 2024 Contribution Limits At A Glance

The IRS announced 2024 limits in late 2023. Most limits increased from 2023. Here are the numbers that matter for most people.

* **401k, 403b, and most 457 plans**: $23,000 (up from $22,500)
* **Catch-up contributions for age 50 and older**: $7,500 (unchanged)
* **Total 401k limit including employer match**: $69,000 (up from $66,000)
* **Traditional and Roth IRA**: $7,000 (up from $6,500)
* **IRA catch-up for age 50 and older**: $1,000 (unchanged)
* **SIMPLE IRA**: $16,000 (up from $15,500)
* **SIMPLE IRA catch-up for age 50 and older**: $3,500 (unchanged)
* **SEP IRA**: $69,000 (up from $66,000) or 25 percent of compensation

These limits apply per person, not per account. You can split contributions between a traditional and Roth 401k. The total cannot exceed $23,000. The same rule applies to IRAs. You can contribute to both types but the combined total stays at $7,000.

## 2025 Contribution Limits Preview

The IRS typically announces next year limits in October or November. Early projections suggest modest increases. Inflation has cooled but the indexing formula still pushes limits higher.

Watch for these potential 2025 changes:
* **401k limit**: Likely $23,500 or $24,000
* **IRA limit**: Likely $7,000 or $7,500
* **Catch-up amounts**: Usually adjust every few years

Bookmark the IRS Newsroom page. They publish official limits as soon as they're finalized. Planning ahead lets you adjust payroll deductions in January instead of playing catch-up in December.

## How Income Affects Your IRA Eligibility

Contribution limits tell you how much you *can* put in. Income limits tell you whether you *get the tax benefit*. This distinction catches many people off guard.

### Traditional IRA Deduction Limits

If you or your spouse has a workplace retirement plan, your deduction phases out at higher incomes.

**Single filers covered by workplace plan:**
* Full deduction: Modified AGI $77,000 or less
* Partial deduction: $77,000 to $87,000
* No deduction: Over $87,000

**Married filing jointly (you covered by workplace plan):**
* Full deduction: Modified AGI $123,000 or less
* Partial deduction: $123,000 to $143,000
* No deduction: Over $143,000

**Married filing jointly (spouse covered, you not covered):**
* Full deduction: Modified AGI $230,000 or less
* Partial deduction: $230,000 to $240,000
* No deduction: Over $240,000

### Roth IRA Income Limits

Roth contributions phase out completely at higher incomes. No workaround exists for direct contributions.

**Single filers:**
* Full contribution: Modified AGI under $146,000
* Partial contribution: $146,000 to $161,000
* No contribution: Over $161,000

**Married filing jointly:**
* Full contribution: Modified AGI under $230,000
* Partial contribution: $230,000 to $240,000
* No contribution: Over $240,000

If your income exceeds these limits, you still have options. The backdoor Roth IRA strategy works for many high earners. You make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution then convert to Roth. No income limits exist for conversions.

## The 401k Catch-Up Provision Gets A Twist

Starting in 2024, a new rule affects catch-up contributions for higher earners. If you earned more than $145,000 in the previous year (indexed for inflation), your catch-up contributions must go to a Roth 401k. You lose the immediate tax deduction on that $7,500.

This applies only to the catch-up portion. Your regular $23,000 can still go pre-tax. The rule takes effect for 2026 contributions. The IRS delayed implementation from 2024 to give plans time to update systems.

If you're under the income threshold, nothing changes. You can still make pre-tax catch-up contributions. If you're over, the Roth requirement means paying taxes now for tax-free growth later. This can actually benefit you if tax rates rise.

## Maximizing Employer Match Before Anything Else

Free money comes first. Always contribute enough to get your full employer match before worrying about IRA limits or catch-up provisions.

A typical match looks like this: 100 percent of your first 4 percent of salary. On a $75,000 salary, that's $3,000 free money. You'd need to contribute $3,000 to get it. That's a 100 percent instant return. No investment matches that.

Check your plan documents for the exact formula. Some plans match 50 percent up to 6 percent. Others use a tiered structure. Missing the match by even one percent leaves hundreds on the table every year.

## When To Use Each Account Type

Account order matters. Follow this priority for most situations:

1. **401k up to employer match** - Free money, immediate return
2. **HSA if eligible** - Triple tax advantage, invest the balance
3. **Roth or traditional IRA** - More investment choices, lower fees
4. **Remaining 401k space** - Higher limits, automatic payroll deduction
5. **Taxable brokerage** - No limits, flexible access, favorable long-term rates

Your specific order might shift. High-fee 401k plans sometimes make IRA the better second choice. No-match 401k plans drop to priority four. Self-employed people prioritize SEP or Solo 401k accounts.

## Common Mistakes That Cost You

### Contributing To The Wrong Account First
Many people max their 401k before getting the full match. Others fund a Roth IRA while their 401k match sits unclaimed. Order matters. Match first. Everything else follows.

### Forgetting Spousal IRA Contributions
Non-working spouses can contribute to an IRA based on the working spouse's income. The limit is $7,000 (or $8,000 if 50+). This doubles household retirement savings for single-income families. Many couples miss this entirely.

### Exceeding The Annual Limit
Contributing too much triggers a 6 percent excise tax every year until fixed. If you have multiple 401k plans from job changes, track the combined total. The limit applies to you, not each plan.

### Ignoring The Mega Backdoor Roth
Some 401k plans allow after-tax contributions above the $23,000 limit. You can then convert those to Roth within the plan or roll to a Roth IRA. This adds up to $46,000 more in tax-free space. Check your plan documents or ask HR.

### Letting Old 401k Accounts Sit
Average job tenure is four years. Most people accumulate several 401k accounts. Consolidating to a single IRA simplifies tracking and often reduces fees. Direct rollovers avoid tax withholding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens if I contribute too much to my 401k?
You have until April 15 of the following year to withdraw the excess plus earnings. The withdrawal gets taxed as income. If you miss the deadline, you pay a 6 percent excise tax annually until corrected. Contact your plan administrator immediately if you notice an overcontribution.

### Can I contribute to both a 401k and an IRA in the same year?
Yes. The limits are separate. You can contribute $23,000 to your 401k and $7,000 to your IRA. Income limits may restrict your IRA tax deduction or Roth eligibility. The contribution itself is always allowed.

### Do employer matching contributions count toward my $23,000 limit?
No. Employer matches count toward the $69,000 total limit. Your personal deferrals stop at $23,000. The match is additional. This is why the total limit matters for high earners with generous matches.

### What if I have two jobs with 401k plans?
Your personal $23,000 limit applies across both plans combined. You must track contributions yourself. Neither employer knows what you contribute elsewhere. Overcontributing triggers penalties. Consider designating one plan for catch-up contributions if you're 50+.

### When do I have to make IRA contributions for the tax year?
You have until the tax filing deadline, typically April 15. Contributions made January through April can apply to the previous year. Specify the tax year when contributing. This gives you extra time to fund your IRA.

### Does a rollover count toward my contribution limit?
No. Direct rollovers from 401k to IRA or between IRAs don't count. Roth conversions don't count either. Only new money from your paycheck or bank account counts toward annual limits.

### What if I turn 50 this year? When can I make catch-up contributions?
You can make catch-up contributions starting the year you turn 50. You don't wait for your birthday. If you turn 50 in December 2024, you can contribute the full $30,500 to your 401k for 2024.

## Key Takeaways

* The 2024 401k limit is $23,000 with a $7,500 catch-up for age 50+
* IRA limits increased to $7,000 with $1,000 catch-up
* Income limits determine tax benefits, not contribution eligibility
* Employer match comes first in your funding priority
* New Roth catch-up rule affects earners over $145,000 starting 2026
* Track combined contributions across all plans to avoid penalties
* Spousal IRA doubles savings for single-income households
* Mega backdoor Roth adds significant tax-free space if your plan allows

## What To Do Next

Log into your 401k portal today. Check your year-to-date contributions. Calculate how much room remains before the $23,000 limit. Increase your deferral percentage if you're not on track to max out by December.

If you're 50 or older, verify catch-up contributions are active. Some plans require a separate election.

Open your IRA provider and schedule automatic monthly contributions. $583 monthly hits the $7,000 limit exactly. $667 monthly hits the $8,000 catch-up limit.

Review your tax return from last year. Note your modified AGI. Compare it to the IRA income limits above. This tells you whether traditional deductions or Roth contributions are available.

Finally, bookmark the IRS retirement plan page. Check it every November for the next year's limits. Adjust your payroll in January. The person who knows the limits builds wealth faster than the person who guesses.
